# Mission Cards — Twelve Chapters, Twelve Teaching Moments

Print one card per chapter. Share with your co-teacher, parent volunteer, or post at the maker station. Each card is ~80 words: the chapter's core skill, three discussion prompts, and one offline activity.

---

## Chapter 1: The Gate Is Never Locked
**Skill:** Salvage fundamentals — what the yard's blocks are, what they drop, why materials matter

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Why does the game give you scrap instead of brand-new parts? What does that teach about engineering in the real world?
2. If you were building your first robot, what would you scavenge first—power, structure, or sensors? Why?
3. The yard lit up at dusk. What moment made you feel like you belonged there?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Scrap Hunt" — Give your kid a room and 10 minutes to collect "building materials" (socks, pencils, paper, blocks). They bring the pile to you. Ask: "What could we BUILD with this?" No wrong answers. This is what engineers do—see potential in waste.

---

## Chapter 2: Earn Your Tools
**Skill:** The crafting pipeline — inputs, stations, tiers; the right tool gates the right job

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Why make your own wrench instead of just finding one? What do you learn from building tools?
2. What's a tool you use every day? Where did it come from, and how would you make it from scratch?
3. Rivet's slow-blink felt like a compliment. What's a small moment that told you someone believed in your work?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Tool Ceremony" — Pick a simple tool (wooden spoon, paintbrush, pencil). Ask your kid to tell its story: What would it take to MAKE this? Where would you get the materials? This builds respect for the crafted object.

---

## Chapter 3: Something With Thumbs
**Skill:** System assembly — actuators, chassis, power; the moment a pile of parts becomes a someone

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. The first boot beep was a big moment. When does a pile of parts become a robot? (Hint: when it moves.)
2. What's harder—building the body or giving it power? Which one makes it "alive"?
3. That six-note anthem played when your bot woke up. What sound would make YOUR bot come to life?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Bring It to Life" — Build something from household items (cardboard, string, tape) that has exactly one job: pick up a penny, ring a bell, or roll across a table. The moment it WORKS, it's alive. Celebrate that moment.

---

## Chapter 4: One Knob at a Time
**Skill:** The feedback loop — sense, think, act; one-variable-at-a-time debugging (the campaign's spine skill)

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. You crashed once, fixed one knob, and it worked. That's the Mellon Loop. When did you use it (even before the game)?
2. What's harder: understanding what's broken, or trusting yourself to change just ONE thing?
3. Earl says "change everything and you'll learn nothing but confusion." Where else is this true?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"One Change at a Time" — Set up a simple challenge (knock over a cup with a ball roll). Kid gets three tries: each time, ONE change only (speed, angle, or height). Which single change worked best? That's the loop.

---

## Chapter 5: The Oval Has Opinions
**Skill:** Closed-loop feedback — line-following, thresholds, why repeatable beats fast

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. June's name went on the board. Does seeing someone else's name make you faster, or just more present?
2. What's the difference between going fast once and going fast EVERY lap?
3. A tenth of a second. How long is that? What's it worth?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"The Oval" — Mark a lap on the floor (tape or chalk). Race a toy car three times and time each lap. How much faster/slower? Consistency or speed—which did you get? What if you tried a third time?

---

## Chapter 6: The Library of Almosts
**Skill:** Sensor interpretation & calibration — the Deep Yard's hazards make sensors matter

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Quill paid you in a crystal that hums when weather turns. How do real sensors "know" things?
2. Why does a sensor need calibration? (Hint: the world is messier than a lab.)
3. That Deep Yard felt different—less mysterious, more real. What changes when you understand the hazards?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Sensor Walk" — Go outside. Point out five things a sensor COULD read: temperature (hot/cold), light (bright/dim), distance (near/far), sound (loud/quiet), moisture (wet/dry). Why would a robot need to know each one?

---

## Chapter 7: The Same Robot, Real
**Skill:** Hardware abstraction — game brain to real firmware; the three-tier ladder (intent → tiles → code)

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Your game program scrolled past as real C++. Does that change how you think about the code?
2. A microcontroller is small and stubborn. What's the difference between a computer brain and a chip brain?
3. Spark vibrated. When does the game feel REAL to you?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"The Three Brains" — Draw three boxes: (1) "What I want the robot to do," (2) "Tiles/blocks I use," (3) "Real code the chip runs." Connect them. This is abstraction—three ways of saying the same thing.

---

## Chapter 8: Fail Loudly
**Skill:** Failure analysis — post-mortems as curriculum; debugging as reading the past

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Every wreck had a plaque. Why light up failures instead of hiding them?
2. Crashes tell stories. What did a crash teach you about your bot, your code, or yourself?
3. That honor-guard floodlight moment. Was failure being celebrated, or was it mourning? Or both?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"The Plaque Ceremony" — Build something simple (marble run, Jenga tower, domino line). When it fails, stop and write one sentence on a card: "It failed because___." Post it. Do it three times. You're building a failure library.

---

## Chapter 9: Candlelight at 11:58
**Skill:** Sensing in low-signal regimes — light thresholds, filtering, night calibration; evidence discipline

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. The candlelight corner with the floods down. What's scarier—the dark, or the things you can't see?
2. A bot racing at night with one light source. Why is that harder than racing in daylight?
3. Goosebumps as gameplay. When did the game stop feeling like a game?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Candlelight Navigation" — Lights off, one candle or flashlight. Your kid navigates to a spot you describe (the couch, the kitchen, etc.). How does sight change? How do they rely on memory?

---

## Chapter 10: The Manifest of Midnights
**Skill:** Data logging & signal analysis — the engineer-as-detective toolkit: compare, timestamp, correlate

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Numbers never lied—treads, ticks, timestamps. How is data like handwriting?
2. Earl lined up chalk with "her" old tire tracks. What was Earl remembering?
3. Evidence gathering. When do you trust data over your gut feeling?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Tire Tracks" — Roll a toy car through flour or sand three times. Measure the tracks—distance, width, depth. Are they identical? Variations? This is data: the world leaves evidence. Read it.

---

## Chapter 11: The Back Room
**Skill:** Integration — multi-system rebuild under constraint; every plaque lesson is one of Mo's rebuild tasks

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Mo's been waiting in the back room for twenty-six years. What are YOU waiting to wake up?
2. A rebuild brings together everything you've learned. What feels hardest to integrate?
3. The moment she chirped. How did you know she was happy?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"The Rebuild" — Give your kid a broken object (old toy, busted remote, etc.) and let them take it apart. No pressure to fix it—just document where each piece goes. Respect the complexity. This is integration.

---

## Chapter 12: The Midnight Race
**Skill:** Everything, integrated — night racing, closed loops, tuned thresholds, race-day power checks

**Field Notes for Discussion:**
1. Twenty-six years on the lap timer. What makes a race moment more than just a lap?
2. Floodlights, midnight, the whole town watching. What's the difference between practice and showtime?
3. The yard knows your name now. What did that feel like?

**Try at Home (Unplugged):**
"Victory Lap" — Set up one final challenge using everything you've learned (or built) this whole time. Not to win—to remember. Then celebrate: why do humans need finish lines? What makes an ending feel like a beginning?

---

## Notes for Teachers

These cards are conversation starters, not tests. The "Try at Home" activities don't need electricity, won't break anything, and work best when you're curious alongside your kid, not correcting them. The field notes are prompts to reveal what they already learned—they're usually surprised.

Print one per chapter. Laminate if you plan to reuse them. Leave blank space on the back for your own notes: moments that landed, questions that surprised you, the small victories nobody saw but you. That's the real data.
